Ask any accountant, shop manager or office assistant which program they open first in the morning, and a lot of them will say Excel. It is not glamorous. But if you can use it well, people notice, and it is one of the few skills that helps in almost every job.

If you are searching for an **MS Excel course in Pakistan**, here is what we think a beginner should learn first, and what to leave for later. We have kept the examples in rupees and in the kind of work a small shop or office actually does.

## First, set the sheet up properly

Before any formula, learn how to lay out data. One row per item, one column per type of information, a heading on top, and no blank rows in the middle. Half of the Excel problems we see in class are not formula problems at all. They come from a messy sheet.

Also learn to freeze the top row, format numbers as currency, and turn your range into a **Table** (Ctrl + T). A table grows by itself as you add rows, and every later step becomes easier.

## The eight things worth learning first

### 1. SUM and AVERAGE

The starting point. If column C has the day's sales, `=SUM(C2:C31)` gives the month's total, and `=AVERAGE(C2:C31)` tells you an ordinary day. Learn the shortcut (Alt + =) as well. You will use it hundreds of times.

### 2. IF

This is where a sheet starts making decisions. Suppose a customer's balance is in column D and you want a flag for anyone who owes more than Rs 50,000:

`=IF(D2>50000,"Follow up","OK")`

That one idea, a question with two possible answers, sits behind a surprising amount of office work.

### 3. COUNTIF and SUMIF

How many orders were cash? How much did one salesman sell?

`=COUNTIF(B2:B100,"Cash")` counts them. `=SUMIF(A2:A100,"Ahmed",C2:C100)` adds up only Ahmed's sales. These two save people from filtering and adding by hand.

### 4. XLOOKUP and VLOOKUP

You have a price list on one sheet and an order on another. A lookup finds the price for each item automatically. VLOOKUP is the older way and you will meet it in many existing files. XLOOKUP is the newer, simpler version. Learn the idea, then use whichever your Excel supports.

### 5. Sorting and filtering

Boring, and very useful. Sort by date, filter one customer, find the ten biggest sales. Most people who say "Excel is hard" are really missing this.

### 6. Charts that explain something

A chart should answer a question. "Are sales going up or down?" is a line chart. "Which product sells most?" is a bar chart. Skip the 3D pie charts. They look busy and are harder to read.

### 7. Pivot tables

The feature that makes people say "oh, that is what it is for." A pivot table summarises a big list in seconds: sales by month, by product or by salesman, just by dragging fields around. It looks scary on the first day and feels natural by the third.

### 8. Printing and sharing properly

Set the print area, fit the sheet to one page wide, and repeat the header row. Then learn to save as PDF. A beautiful sheet that prints across five pages is a very common office embarrassment.

## What can wait

Macros, Power Query and complicated array formulas are real skills, but they are not first-month skills. Learn them when your work actually needs them. Most beginners who rush ahead end up with fragile sheets they cannot fix.

## Common mistakes we see

- **Merging cells everywhere.** It looks tidy and breaks sorting, filtering and pivot tables.
- **Typing numbers as text,** like "Rs 5,000" in the cell. Excel cannot add that. Keep the number plain and format it instead.
- **Colouring cells to carry meaning.** If the colour is the only place a fact lives, you cannot count it. Add a column.
- **Hard-coding numbers inside formulas.** Put a rate in its own cell and point to it.

## How to practise

Use your own data. A home budget, a shop's stock list, class attendance. Real numbers with real mistakes teach you more than tidy practice files. If you can, rebuild something you currently do by hand, and time yourself before and after.
